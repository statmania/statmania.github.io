The Best Order to Write a Research Paper

Why writing a paper top-to-bottom causes writer’s block, and the inside-out sequence experienced researchers use instead.
research

Author

Abdullah Al Mahmud

Published

September 11, 2026

A final paper is always read from Introduction to Conclusion, but writing it in that order is inefficient. Starting with the introduction often causes writer’s block, because the narrative’s final nuances aren’t clear yet — you don’t fully know what story you’re introducing until you’ve analyzed your own results.

Experienced researchers instead use an inside-out sequence: start with the easiest, most concrete, data-driven sections and move toward the harder, more abstract ones.

The Recommended Inside-Out Sequence

Step Section Why This Order Works
1 Figures, Tables & Data Visualizations The core of the work. Organizing graphs and tables first creates a structural roadmap for everything you write after.
2 Methods The easiest section — it just describes what you did. Often draftable straight from lab notes or protocols, switched to past tense.
3 Results Describe findings objectively based on the figures from Step 1: facts, percentages, and trends — without interpreting what they mean yet.
4 Discussion Interpret the results, explain why they matter, note limitations, and compare against existing literature.
5 Introduction Now that you fully understand your findings and how they fit the field, framing the “gap in knowledge” your paper bridges is easy.
6 Conclusion & References Summarize the main takeaway, suggest future work, and tidy up citations with a reference manager.
7 Abstract & Title Always last — you can’t compress an entire paper into a tight summary until the full text is finished.

Phase 1: The Core (Data & Mechanics)

  • Figures & Tables: Lay out visual data in a logical sequence that tells a story, even if it differs from the chronological order the experiments were run in.
  • Methods: Break this into standard subsections — study design, materials/population, data collection procedures, and statistical analysis.

Phase 2: The Narrative (Interpretation)

  • Results: Stick strictly to facts. Use phrasing like “Figure 1 demonstrates that…” or “Group A exhibited a 12% increase compared to Group B.”
  • Discussion: Structure paragraphs with the TEEL formula:
    • Topic sentence: state the core claim or finding.
    • Explain: elaborate on what the data implies.
    • Evidence: cite prior studies that agree or disagree with the outcome.
    • Link: connect the paragraph back to the overall research question.

Phase 3: The Frame (Contextualizing)

  • Introduction: Use a funnel approach — start broad with the big-picture problem, narrow to the specific gap in current literature, and end with the specific hypothesis or objective.
  • Abstract: Package the paper by pulling one or two sentences each from the Introduction, Methods, Results, and Conclusion into a stand-alone summary.

Takeaway

Write in the order you can answer questions with confidence: what you did, what you found, what it means, and only then why it matters to the field. The introduction and abstract come last because they’re summaries of a story you can’t yet tell until the rest is written.
